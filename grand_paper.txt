ABSTRACT
Graph retrieval aims to find the most similar graphs in a graph
database given a query graph, which is a fundamental problem
with many real-world applications in chemical engineering, code
analysis, etc. To date, existing neural graph retrieval methods gener-
ally fall into two categories: Embedding Based Paradigm (Ebp) and
Matching Based Paradigm (Mbp). The Ebp models learn an individ-
ual vectorial representation for each graph and the retrieval process
can be accelerated by pre-computing these representations. The
Mbp models learn a neural matching function to compare graphs on
a pair-by-pair basis, in which the fine-grained pairwise comparison
leads to higher retrieval accuracy but severely degrades retrieval
efficiency. In this paper, to combine the advantage of Ebp in re-
trieval efficiency with that of Mbp in retrieval accuracy, we propose
a novel Graph RetrievAl framework via KNowledge Distillation,
namely Grand. The key point is to leverage the idea of knowledge
distillation to transfer the fine-grained graph comparison knowl-
edge from an Mbp model to an Ebp model, such that the Ebp model
can generate better graph representations and thus yield higher
retrieval accuracy. At the same time, we can still pre-compute and
index the improved graph representations to retain the retrieval

A common approach for graph retrieval is to scan the entire
graph database and find the most similar graphs to a query graph.
Traditional methods rely on various graph similarity/distance mea-
sures such as Graph Edit Distance (GED) and Maximum Common
Subgraph (MCS). However, it is known that the computation of
GED and MCS is NP-hard, which incurs high computational over-
head, especially when dealing with large graph databases. In recent
years, neural graph retrieval [2, 10, 21] has attracted widespread
attention from both academia and industry, which aims to learn
a neural network to compute the similarity between graphs and
allows fast similarity computation after training. Benefiting from
end-to-end learning from data, neural methods are able to perform
retrieval based on human-labeled relevance information in addition
to graph proximity measures such as GED. In this paper, we focus
on the scope of neural graph retrieval.
Existing neural graph retrieval methods can be divided into two
categories: embedding based and matching based paradigms. Fig-
ure 1 gives an overall view of these two paradigms. The embedding
based paradigm (Ebp) maps two input graphs into a common latent
space and computes their similarity score between vectorial graph
representations, while the matching based paradigm (Mbp) learns
neural matching functions to compute the similarity score between
two graphs. Generally speaking, Mbp provides better retrieval accu-
racy than Ebp owing to that Mbp builds some low-level matching
signals (e.g., cross-graph similarity matrices) and captures more
fine-grained interaction patterns across graphs via the matching
function (e.g., deep neural networks). However, Mbp’s retrieval
speed is too slow for practical use because it needs to pair a query
graph with each candidate graph and feed all these pairs through
the entire model. On the other hand, Ebp’s retrieval speed is much
faster because we can compute candidate graphs’ representations
in advance and thus the retrieval problem can be considered as a
nearest neighbor search problem in the corresponding vector space.
Overview of Our Framework. A natural question to ask is how
to combine the advantage of Ebp in terms of retrieval efficiency
with that of Mbp in terms of retrieval accuracy. To answer this
question, we propose a novel fast and accurate Graph RetrievAl
framework via kNowledge Distillation, namely Grand. The key
point is that we leverage the idea of knowledge distillation (KD) to
bridge Ebp and Mbp. Given that Mbp is able to capture richer and
more fine-grained matching patterns between two graphs, we con-
sider Mbp and Ebp as the teacher and student, respectively, and try
to distill these patterns (hereafter also called dark knowledge) from
Mbp to Ebp. Specifically, we define three types of dark knowledge,
namely score-level, node-level, and subgraph-level knowledge, and
perform knowledge transfer1 at these three different levels. In ad-
dition, considering the architecture difference between Mbp and
Ebp as shown in Fig. 1, we argue that Mbp is better at capturing
fine-grained similarity information between graphs via the pair-
wise matching function while Ebp is better at capturing high-level
similarity information by generating global graph embeddings. In
principle, they should be mutually beneficial. Thus, we propose to

After distillation, we expect to obtain anEbp model that can provide

To sum up, our contributions are as follows:
• We formally summarize two typical paradigms for graph retrieval:
Ebp and Mbp, and bridge the two paradigms to improve the trade-
off between accuracy and efficiency.
• We propose a novel graph retrieval framework Grand to bridge
Ebp and Mbp through knowledge distillation. Specifically, we
perform knowledge transfer at different topological scales and
carry out a mutual two-way KD such that Ebp and Mbp can
complement and benefit each other during training.
• We conduct extensive experiments on three real-world datasets.
The empirical results show that our Grand improves the perfor-
mance of Ebp models by a large margin and the improvement
is consistent for different combinations of Ebp and Mbp models.
For example, Grand achieves performance gains of mostly over
10% and up to 16.88% in terms of Recall@𝐾 on different datasets.
Additionally and interestingly, we observe that Grand also im-
proves the performance of Mbp models owing to the mutual
two-way KD. Moreover, we provide several ablation studies to
demonstrate the effectiveness of the design choices in Grand.
2 RELATED WORK
2.1 Traditional Graph Retrieval
This line of work, also known as graph similarity search, retrieves
graphs based on predefined graph similarity measures, such as
GED [30, 44], MCS [3, 31], missing edges and features [40, 43, 45, 50],
graph alignment [34], and graph kernels [26]. Among them, GED is
one of the most commonly used measures due to its generality, and
thus most traditional works focus on GED based graph retrieval.
Given the NP-hardness of the computation of GED, existing so-
lutions are typically developed within a filtering-and-verification
framework to speed up the retrieval process. Inspired by the 𝑞-
gram idea for string similarity search [35], most filtering techniques
build an inverted index on specially designed𝑞-gram features of
graphs and compute GED lower bounds by exploiting matching
and mismatching𝑞-gram features, exemplified by tree-structured
𝑞-grams [37, 44], path-based 𝑞-grams [49], and graph partition
based 𝑞-grams [22, 48]. In addition, there are also some works
that compute GED lower bounds in pairs without indexing. For
example, BSS_GED [5] derives a GED lower bound based on the
vertex degrees of two graphs to be compared.Inves [19] proposes a
bridge-based lower bound estimation technique that incrementally
partitions a graph based on the query graph and uses the results to
compute a lower bound of their GED. In principle, these algorithms
can be used in both the filtering and verification phases owing to
the pairwise computation.
However, traditional graph retrieval methods like the above are
limited in two ways. First, they are still too slow to retrieve from
large graph databases as shown in [29]. Second, these methods are
built upon explicit handcrafted structural features and are dedicated
to specific applications (e.g., where GED is of primary interest), and
thus fail to perform retrieval according to human-labeled relevance
information.
2.2 Neural Graph Retrieval
Neural graph retrieval has received considerable attention in re-
cent years. The core of it is to learn a neural model to compute
the similarity score between two graphs. Generally, the existing
approaches can be grouped into two paradigms: Ebp and Mbp.
Ebp. Ebp learns an individual graph representation for each graph
and computes the similarity between the representations of two
graphs as their graph similarity. A recent trend is to use graph
neural networks (GNNs) to learn graph representations and various
GNN models have been proposed, such as MPNN [9], GCN [20],
GraphSage [11], GAT [36], and GIN [39]. More recent work is
GHashing [29], which further learns a GNN based hash function
to generate binary graph representations, allowing faster graph
similarity search with Hamming distance.
Mbp. Compared with Ebp, the main innovation of Mbp lies in the
use of a matching function to capture fine-grained similarity in-
formation on a pair-by-pair basis. For instance, GMN [
21] uses a
cross-graph node-wise attention mechanism to generate mutually
dependent node representations for each pair of graphs, which are
further transformed into the corresponding graph representations
to obtain the final similarity score. Ling et al. [ 23, 24] further ex-
tend GMN by introducing cross-graph node-graph attention mecha-
nisms. SimGNN [1] combines graph representations with histogram
features extracted from pairwise node similarity scores across two
graphs to compute their similarity score. GraphSim [2] constructs
a node-level similarity matrix and directly feeds it into a convolu-
tional neural network to predict the similarity score between two
graphs. H2MN [47] converts each graph to a hypergraph and then
designs a corresponding cross-hypergraph attention mechanism to
predict the similarity between two graphs. In summary, different
Mbp models mainly differ in the design of the matching function.
2.3 Research Involving Graph Comparison
Recently, significant interest has been devoted to solving several
problems involving graph comparison in an end-to-end learning-
based manner, in addition to the problem of graph retrieval or
graph similarity learning studied in this paper. For example, some
works [7, 8, 13] study the problem of graph alignment, which aims
to find node correspondences between two graphs; some works [6,
25, 42] study the problem of subgraph matching or counting, of
which the goal is to find or count a certain subgraph structure
in a target graph. These problems are related to ours but are not
the same. In our setting, we query a graph database to find the
most similar graphs to an input graph. In the future, it would be
interesting to see if some insights into these related problems can
inspire better solutions to the problem of graph retrieval.

4 THE PROPOSED FRAMEWORK
In this section, we present our proposed graph retrieval framework
Grand in detail, which distills dark knowledge from Mbp to Ebp
from three perspectives: score-level, node-level, and subgraph-level
knowledge distillation. See Fig. 1 for a schematic illustration. In
the following, we first elaborate on the three types of distillation
schemes. Then, we give the overall optimization process of Grand.
Finally, we discuss how to applyGrand to practical graph retrieval
systems, followed by the time complexity analysis of Grand. Here-
after, for ease of exposition, we use the superscripts or subscripts
“Ebp” and “Mbp” to indicate the notations related to the embedding
based and matching based paradigms, respectively.

4.3 Optimization
In this subsection, we discuss the learning strategies to optimize
our framework Grand.
Mutual KD. Traditionally, one-way KD is mainly used for model
compression to transfer the knowledge of a large pre-trained teacher
network (e.g., a 101-layer ResNet) to a small student network (e.g.,
an 18-layer ResNet). In this paper, what we are interested in is the
difference between the overall architecture of Mbp and Ebp, which
leads that Mbp can capture more fine-grained similarity informa-
tion between two graphs through the pairwise matching function
while Ebp is better at capturing high-level similarity via global
graph embeddings. Drawing from this, we argue that Mbp and Ebp
complement each other and should be mutually beneficial. How-
ever, the one-way KD keeps Mbp unchanged and only performs
one-way knowledge transfer from Mbp to Ebp, making it impos-
sible for Mbp to obtain feedback from the learning state of Ebp
and further improve the learning process. Inspired by [46], we pro-
pose to jointly optimize Mbp and Ebp to enable mutual (two-way)
knowledge transfer between them instead. Mathematically, instead
of performing pre-training using the ranking loss, we define the

4.4 Application to Graph Retrieval Systems
After optimization, we obtain an Ebp model𝑓Ebp that can produce
powerful graph representations for graph retrieval with the support
of the knowledge from Mbp. Given the set of candidate graphsDC
to be retrieved, we first useΦnode and Φgraph of𝑓Ebp to compute the
graph representations g𝑐 of all graphsG𝑐∈DC which can be done
in advance or offline. Then, when it comes to a query graph G𝑞,
we similarly compute its representation g𝑞, and retrieve the top-𝐾
candidate graphs according to the similarity between g𝑞 and each
candidate graph’s representationg𝑐. The similarity is defined by the
similarity metric Φsim in𝑓Ebp, which is usually negative Euclidean
distance or cosine (dot product) similarity.
Moreover, in practice, there are basically two kinds of retrieval
strategies: the re-ranking and the full ranking. For the re-ranking
strategy, the Ebp model learned by our Grand can serve as the
fast first-stage retriever to fetch a small set of candidate graphs,
and then a more powerful but slow second-stage retriever (e.g., an
Mbp model) re-ranks these candidate graphs to obtain the top-𝐾
similar graphs. For the full ranking strategy, the Ebp model learned
by Grand can be used to retrieve the top-𝐾 similar graphs directly
from the entire candidate graphs owing to its good trade-off be-
tween retrieval accuracy and efficiency.
4.5 Time Complexity Analysis
Since Grand does not make any changes to the internals of Ebp,
once Grand finishes optimization, we use the resulting Ebp model
to perform graph retrieval as usual. As discussed in § 4.4, we cast
the retrieval problem as a problem of similar vector search. The
naive retrieval solution’s time complexity is 𝑂(𝑁𝑑), where 𝑁 is
the number of candidate graphs and 𝑑 is the graph representa-
tion’s dimension. For large-scale graph retrieval, we can further
employ off-the-shelf efficient vector similarity search tools, such as
faiss [17], to speed up the retrieval process.

5.1 Experimental Setup
5.1.1 Datasets. Three publicly available real-world datasets are
used in our experiments. Their statistics are summarized in Table 1.
• AIDS [2]. This is an antiviral screening compound dataset from
the Developmental Therapeutics Program at NCI/NIH, which
has been a popular benchmark for graph similarity search [ 1,
2, 37, 48]. It originally consists of 42, 687 chemical compounds,
each represented as a graph with atoms as nodes and chemical
bonds as edges. Following [1, 2], we use a subset of the dataset,
which contains 700 graphs and the true GED between each pair of
graphs obtained by the exponential-time exact GED computation
algorithm A∗ [12]. We use one-hot encodings of atom types as
node features. Besides, for each graph, we regard the graphs with
the minimum GED as similar graphs with respect to it.
• CCI. This dataset is constructed based on the chemical-chemical
interactions from the STITCH database (version 5.0) [ 32]. The
STITCH database contains a set of interaction pairs of chemi-
cal compounds, and each interaction pair is associated with an
integer confidence score ranging from 0 to 999. We consider
two compounds with a confidence score more than 800 to be
similar, and dissimilar otherwise. In addition, the database gives
SMILES strings of different chemical compounds. We use RDKit
to convert the SMILES string of each compound to a graph of
which nodes are atoms, edges are chemical bonds, and input node
features are one-hot encodings of atom types.
• FFmpeg [23]. This is a collection of Control-Flow Graphs (CFGs)
of binary functions compiled fromFFmpeg. This dataset uses GCC
v5.4 and Clang v3.8 to compile FFmpeg with 4 different compiler
optimization levels (O0-O3). We consider two CFGs compiled
from the same source code function to be similar, and dissimilar
otherwise. In a CFG, nodes represent basic blocks of assembly in-
structions and edges represent jumps in the control flow, and each
node is associated with a 6-dimensional feature vector derived
from six block-level attributes (i.e., String Constants, Numeric
Constants, No. of Instructions, No. of Transfer Instructions, No.
of Call Instructions, and No. of Arithmetic Instructions). In ad-
dition, we row-normalize input node feature vectors for each
graph.
5.1.2 Baselines. Our main contribution is to propose a framework
Grand to bridge Ebp and Mbp via knowledge distillation. To verify
the effectiveness of Grand, we choose several representative Ebp
and Mbp models as baselines, and investigate different combinations
1644\nGrand: A Fast and Accurate Graph Retrieval Framework via Knowledge Distillation SIGIR ’24, July 14–18, 2024, Washington, DC, USA
within our framework. Specifically, in our experiments, we consider
the following baselines:
• (Ebp) GCN [20] uses a localized first-order approximation of
spectral graph convolutions to learn node embeddings. Then, we
use the same way as GEM to learn graph embeddings.
• (Ebp) GEM [21] uses message passing to learn node embeddings
and then uses a gated MLP followed by sum graph pooling as
well as another MLP to learn graph embeddings.
• (Mbp) GMN [21] is an extension of GEM. It introduces a cross-
graph node-wise attention mechanism to message passing in
order to learn graph-pair-specific node embeddings and then
uses the same way as GEM to learn graph embeddings.
• (Mbp) GraphSim [2] first uses a GNN to learn node embeddings.
Then, it constructs a cross-graph node-wise similarity matrix,
which is fed into a CNN followed by an MLP to predict the
final similarity score between two graphs. Note that GraphSim
is an improved version of SimGNN [1] and thus we omit the
comparison with SimGNN here.
• (KD) EGSC [28] is a graph similarity computation framework
via Knowledge Distillation. It introduces an early-feature fusion
model that takes the GIN as the backbone and proposes a novel
Knowledge Distillation method where the joint embeddings are
decomposed to distill. Unlike our framework which can be used
for any two GNN models, ECSC is only used to distill its designed
models.
• (KD) CPF [41] is a knowledge distillation framework that uses
their knowledge distillation method to distill their designed stu-
dent model in node classification. To compare their distillation
methods, we added soft labels for node classification during train-
ing for distilling node type knowledge.
In principle, our framework Grand is general enough to be applied
to any two models respectively conforming to Ebp and Mbp. Please
note that GHashing [29] improves Ebp by learning binary graph
representations to use Hamming distance to further accelerate the
retrieval speed, while Grand improves Ebp by transferring knowl-
edge from Mbp to Ebp to reach higher retrieval accuracy. Since
Grand and GHashing are developed from different perspectives
with different goals, we do not include GHashing as a baseline
here. Technically, they should be complementary to each other, and
we leave their combination as future work.
5.1.3 Parameter Settings. We implement our Grand and the
involved Ebp and Mbp models in TensorFlow, and conduct all the
experiments on a server with 2 Intel Xeon Gold 6140 CPUs and
8 Nvidia Tesla V100 GPUs. For GCN, we use a three-layer GCN
with the number of hidden units as 128 and ReLU nonlinearity, and
use the same graph encoder as GEM. For GEM and GMN, we use
the network architecture hyperparameters recommended in the
original paper [21]. For GraphSim, we use the same node encoder
as GEM for better performance. The matching function of Graph-
Sim is a five-layer CNN followed by a five-layer MLP on the AIDS
dataset, and a six-layer CNN followed by a seven-layer MLP on
the CCI and FFmpeg datasets. Since our focus is to bridge Ebp and
Mbp, we do not put much effort into tuning the hyperparameters
of the above models. For Grand, we perform a grid search over
𝛼∈{ 0.25, 0.5, 0.75} and𝛽,𝛾 ∈{ 0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10} As for the tem-
perature parameters𝜏score,𝜏 node,𝜏 subgraph, we refer to the values
in the original paper [14].
For all datasets and models, we use the Adam optimizer with
a learning rate of 0.001 to perform mini-batch training with early
stopping. The batch sizes are set to 32, 64, 64, and 128 for the
AIDS, CCI, and FFmpeg datasets, respectively. We set the number
of training iterations to 30, 000 for the AIDS dataset and 120, 000
for the CCI, and FFmpeg datasets.

6 CONCLUSION
To combine the advantage of Ebp in retrieval efficiency with that
of Mbp in retrieval accuracy, this paper presents a novel graph re-
trieval framework Grand that bridges Ebp and Mbp via knowledge
distillation. Specifically, our Grand performs knowledge transfer
from three perspectives: score-level, node-level, and subgraph-level
KD. Furthermore, in addition to the vanilla one-way KD from Mbp
to Ebp, our Grand also proposes to apply mutual two-way KD
between Ebp and Mbp enabling Ebp and Mbp to complement and
benefit each other during optimization. Extensive experiments on
three real-world datasets demonstrate the superiority of ourGrand.
